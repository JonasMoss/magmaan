\section*{Purpose}

The Guttman estimator used by \texttt{magmaan} is best understood as a
covariance-summary map, not as the minimizer of the usual CFA covariance
discrepancy. It does contain a clean minimization step, but that step is a
regression of observed variables on fixed indicator composites. The CFA
parameters are then read directly from the regression on standardized
composites.

This note records the construction in enough detail to audit an implementation.
It also states which discrepancy is natural for multi-sample constraints. The
answer is:
\begin{itemize}
\item the construction itself minimizes a composite-score prediction error;
\item NTML and ULS discrepancies evaluated at the Guttman estimate are valid
  residual statistics, but not native likelihood-ratio statistics;
\item the native constrained or multi-sample test is a minimum-distance or Wald
  statistic in the estimator's own asymptotic covariance metric.
\end{itemize}

Here ``Guttman estimator'' means Guttman's historical multiple-group method for
common-factor analysis \cite{guttman1952}. The historical phrase
``multiple-group'' names groups of indicators used to form composites. It is not
multi-group SEM. Multi-group SEM enters later by stacking independent sample
blocks.

The current \texttt{magmaan} frontier surface deliberately keeps two named
versions. The selector \texttt{guttman} is the legacy, lavaan-like
Spearman/incidence version: it uses the average-ratio Spearman diagonal with the
wide communality bounds inherited from the start-value path, and then uses the
incidence composites \(Z\). The promoted research selector
\texttt{guttman\_gls\_aligned} uses the blockwise triad-GMM diagonal estimator
for \(H\) and replaces the fixed incidence scores by the aligned score
reconstruction described below. Both are still maps
\(S\mapsto H\mapsto(\Lambda,\Phi,\psi)\), and both use the same
finite-difference \(J\) for delta-method standard errors and residual tests.

\section{Single-block setup}

Fix one continuous CFA block with \(p\) observed variables and \(k\) factors.
Let \(S\) be the sample covariance and let
\[
  Z\in\{0,1\}^{p\times k}
\]
be the indicator-by-factor incidence matrix. In the simple-structure case used
by the current estimator, each row of \(Z\) has exactly one nonzero entry:
\(Z_{if}=1\) means that observed variable \(i\) is assigned to factor \(f\).
Write \(f(i)\) for that assigned factor, and let \(m_f\) denote the marker
indicator for factor \(f\).

The estimator is defined only at regular points. Each factor must have at least
three indicators, the within-factor correlations used below must avoid zero
denominators, the composite variances must be positive, and the standardized
composite covariance must be positive definite. A first-loading-equals-one
conversion additionally requires nonzero marker loadings.

\section{Step 1: replace variances by communalities}

The first step creates a covariance-like matrix \(H\) whose off-diagonal entries
are the observed covariances and whose diagonal entries are estimated common
variances. Write \(R\) for the correlation matrix of \(S\). For an indicator
\(i\), let
\[
  P_i
  =
  \{(j,\ell): j<\ell,\ j\ne i,\ \ell\ne i,\ f(j)=f(\ell)=f(i)\}.
\]
The Spearman-Harman correlation-scale communality is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spearman-h}
  \hat h_i^{\mathrm{cor}}
  =
  |P_i|^{-1}
  \sum_{(j,\ell)\in P_i}
    \frac{R_{ij}R_{i\ell}}{R_{j\ell}} .
\end{equation}
Convert it back to covariance scale by
\[
  \hat h_i = S_{ii}\hat h_i^{\mathrm{cor}},
  \qquad
  \hat u_i = S_{ii}-\hat h_i ,
\]
and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H}
  H
  =
  S-\diag(S)+\diag(\hat h)
  =
  S-\diag(\hat u).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Exact recovery of the diagonal common variance]
\label{lem:spearman}
Suppose \(i,j,\ell\) are indicators of the same one-dimensional factor, with
population covariances
\[
  \Sigma_{ab}=\lambda_a\lambda_b\phi
  \qquad (a\ne b).
\]
Then every ratio in \eqref{eq:spearman-h} equals
\[
  \lambda_i^2\phi / \Sigma_{ii}
\]
on the correlation scale, and therefore \(h_i=\lambda_i^2\phi\) on the
covariance scale.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
On the covariance scale,
\[
  \frac{\Sigma_{ij}\Sigma_{i\ell}}{\Sigma_{j\ell}}
  =
  \frac{(\lambda_i\lambda_j\phi)(\lambda_i\lambda_\ell\phi)}
       {\lambda_j\lambda_\ell\phi}
  =
  \lambda_i^2\phi .
\]
Dividing each covariance by the relevant standard deviations gives the
correlation-scale identity and multiplying back by \(\Sigma_{ii}\) returns the
same covariance-scale common variance.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The smooth asymptotic notes treat the interior map, where no numerical repair is
active. The shared \texttt{theta\_spearman()} helper used by the current C++
code clips the correlation-scale communality to wide bounds before converting
to a residual variance. The frontier estimator rejects factors with fewer than
three indicators before the helper's small-block fallback can matter. If a
bound is active, the map is piecewise smooth rather than smooth at that point,
and the finite-difference Jacobian is differentiating that active branch.
\end{remark}

\section{Step 2: regress variables on standardized composites}

This step is a population regression problem. Let \(Y_H\) be a centered
auxiliary \(p\)-vector with covariance
\[
  \Cov(Y_H)=H .
\]
The subscript is only a reminder: \(Y_H\) is not new observed data, but a
population device for writing the least-squares problem whose second moments
are the communality-filled matrix \(H\). If \(H\) is not positive semidefinite,
the same formulas still define a quadratic algebraic map, but the literal
random-vector interpretation is lost.

The raw indicator composites are
\[
  U=Z\T Y_H .
\]
Let
\[
  D=\diag(Z\T HZ),
\]
and assume the diagonal entries of \(D\) are positive. The standardized
indicator composites are
\[
  F=D^{-1/2}U=D^{-1/2}Z\T Y_H .
\]
Their covariance is the composite correlation matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PhiG}
  \Phi_G
  =
  \Cov(F)
  =
  D^{-1/2}(Z\T HZ)D^{-1/2}.
\end{equation}
Thus \(\diag(\Phi_G)=I\). In this parameterization, \(\Phi_G\) is the
unit-variance latent covariance. It is not introduced after the regression; it
is the covariance of the standardized fixed composites used in the regression.

For fixed \(F\), choose a loading matrix \(\Lambda\in\mathbb R^{p\times k}\) to
minimize the population prediction error
\begin{equation}\label{eq:composite-loss}
  \Lambda_G
  =
  \argmin_{\Lambda\in\mathbb R^{p\times k}}
  \E_H\{ \lVert Y_H-\Lambda F\rVert^2 \}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the objective is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:composite-objective}
  Q_{\mathrm{comp}}(\Lambda)
  =
  \tr(H)-2\tr\{\Lambda D^{-1/2}Z\T H\}
  +\tr(\Lambda\Phi_G\Lambda\T).
\end{equation}
The subscript \(H\) emphasizes that the expectation uses only the
communality-filled second moments. Row \(i\) of \(\Lambda_G\) is the population least
squares coefficient vector in the regression of \(Y_{H,i}\) on the fixed
standardized composites \(F\). The stacked problem in \eqref{eq:composite-loss} is just
the sum of those \(p\) regressions.

\begin{lemma}[Composite regression step]\label{lem:regression}
If \(\Phi_G\) is positive definite, the unique minimizer of
\eqref{eq:composite-loss} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LambdaG}
  \Lambda_G = HZD^{-1/2}\Phi_G^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The common covariance reproduced by the composites is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:common-proj}
  \Lambda_G\Phi_G\Lambda_G\T
  =
  H Z (Z\T H Z)^{-1} Z\T H .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate \eqref{eq:composite-objective} with respect to \(\Lambda\):
\[
  \frac{\partial Q_{\mathrm{comp}}}{\partial \Lambda}
  =
  -2HZD^{-1/2}+2\Lambda\Phi_G .
\]
The first-order condition is \(\Lambda\Phi_G=HZD^{-1/2}\), giving
\(\Lambda_G=HZD^{-1/2}\Phi_G^{-1}\). Since \(\Phi_G\) is positive definite,
this stationary point is the unique minimum. Substituting
\(\Phi_G=D^{-1/2}(Z\T HZ)D^{-1/2}\) gives \eqref{eq:common-proj}.
\end{proof}

This is the actual least-squares part of the Guttman estimator. The variables
being optimized are the loadings from standardized fixed indicator composites
to the observed variables. The unit-variance latent covariance is simply the
correlation matrix of those composites.

The residual variances are read from the diagonal residual covariance:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psiG}
  \psi_G=\diag\{S-\Lambda_G\Phi_G\Lambda_G\T\}.
\end{equation}
The full \(\Lambda_G\) is computed by the regression. The reported free loading
parameters are read only from the model-allowed loading cells. Off-pattern
entries of \(\Lambda_G\) are auxiliary. They vanish on the exact
simple-structure model; away from that model they are not fitted as
cross-loadings.

If first-loading-equals-one output is desired, it is only a diagonal
reparameterization of the unit-variance result. Let
\[
  M_{\mathrm{fl}}
  =
  \diag\{(\Lambda_G)_{m_1,1},\ldots,(\Lambda_G)_{m_k,k}\}.
\]
Then
\[
  \Lambda_{\mathrm{fl}}=\Lambda_GM_{\mathrm{fl}}^{-1},
  \qquad
  \Phi_{\mathrm{fl}}=M_{\mathrm{fl}}\Phi_GM_{\mathrm{fl}},
  \qquad
  \psi_{\mathrm{fl}}=\psi_G .
\]
This conversion is estimator-independent and requires the chosen marker
loadings to be nonzero.

\begin{proposition}[Exact recovery on the unit-variance simple CFA model]\label{prop:recover}
Suppose the communality-filled population matrix is exactly
\[
  H_0=\Lambda_0\Phi_0\Lambda_0\T ,
\]
where \(\Lambda_0\) has the simple loading pattern encoded by \(Z\),
\(\diag(\Phi_0)=I\), and the diagonal matrix \(T=\Lambda_0\T Z\) is
nonsingular. Let \(S_T=\diag\{\operatorname{sign}(T_{11}),\ldots,
\operatorname{sign}(T_{kk})\}\). Then the unit-variance Guttman map gives
\[
  \Lambda_G=\Lambda_0S_T,
  \qquad
  \Phi_G=S_T\Phi_0S_T .
\]
After orienting each latent factor so \(T_{ff}>0\), it recovers
\(\Lambda_0\) and \(\Phi_0\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Because every indicator loads on one factor, \(T=\Lambda_0\T Z\) is diagonal.
Thus
\[
  H_0Z=\Lambda_0\Phi_0T,
  \qquad
  Z\T H_0Z=T\Phi_0T .
\]
Since \(\diag(\Phi_0)=I\), the composite variance matrix is
\[
  D=\diag(Z\T H_0Z)=T^2 .
\]
Therefore
\[
  D^{-1/2}T=S_T,
  \qquad
  \Phi_G=D^{-1/2}(Z\T H_0Z)D^{-1/2}=S_T\Phi_0S_T .
\]
The variable-composite covariance on the standardized scale is
\[
  H_0ZD^{-1/2}=\Lambda_0\Phi_0S_T .
\]
The regression loading matrix is
\[
  H_0ZD^{-1/2}\Phi_G^{-1}
  =
  \Lambda_0\Phi_0S_T(S_T\Phi_0S_T)^{-1}
  =
  \Lambda_0S_T .
\]
\end{proof}

\section{General composite matrices and equivariance}

The incidence matrix \(Z\) can be replaced by a fixed composite matrix
\[
  A\in\mathbb R^{p\times k}.
\]
This is a useful factor-score-shaped construction, but it is not an ordinary
factor score estimate from the raw data covariance. The scores below are
composites of the auxiliary vector \(Y_H\), whose covariance is \(H\). Thus
\(A\T Y_H\) should be read as fixed score proxies in the communality-filled
population problem.

For a fixed \(A\), define
\[
  U_A=A\T Y_H,
  \qquad
  D_A=\diag(A\T H A),
  \qquad
  F_A=D_A^{-1/2}A\T Y_H .
\]
Assuming the diagonal of \(D_A\) is positive, set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general-composite-map}
  \Phi_A=D_A^{-1/2}(A\T H A)D_A^{-1/2},
  \qquad
  \Lambda_A=HAD_A^{-1/2}\Phi_A^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The original Guttman map is the special case \(A=Z\). The matrix
\(AD_A^{-1/2}\) plays the role of a fixed score-coefficient matrix in the
auxiliary \(H\)-world, and \(\Lambda_A\) is the population regression of
\(Y_H\) on those standardized score proxies.

The exact-recovery condition becomes a condition on the score proxies. Suppose
\[
  H_0=\Lambda_0\Phi_0\Lambda_0\T,
  \qquad
  \diag(\Phi_0)=I,
\]
and let \(T_A=\Lambda_0\T A\). If \(T_A\) is diagonal and nonsingular, then
the columns of \(A\T Y_H\) are factor-pure proxies. With
\[
  S_A=\diag\{\operatorname{sign}((T_A)_{11}),\ldots,
              \operatorname{sign}((T_A)_{kk})\},
\]
the same calculation as in Proposition \ref{prop:recover} gives
\[
  \Phi_A=S_A\Phi_0S_A,
  \qquad
  \Lambda_A=\Lambda_0S_A .
\]
Thus \(A\) need not be a 0/1 incidence matrix. What matters for exact recovery
is that its score proxies isolate the latent axes.

There are two useful ways to construct such matrices.

First, if the population loading matrix were known and had full column rank, an
oracle choice is
\[
  A_{\mathrm{or}}
  =
  \Lambda_0(\Lambda_0\T\Lambda_0)^{-1}.
\]
Then \(\Lambda_0\T A_{\mathrm{or}}=I\). This shows that many exact-recovery
composite matrices exist, but it is not an estimator because it uses the
unknown \(\Lambda_0\).

Second, the pattern-only construction uses factor-pure anchors. Let
\[
  \mathcal P_f
  =
  \{i:\hbox{ indicator } i \hbox{ is allowed to load on factor } f
      \hbox{ and on no other factor}\}.
\]
Choose a nonzero vector \(a_f\in\mathbb R^p\) supported only on
\(\mathcal P_f\), and set
\[
  A=(a_1,\ldots,a_k).
\]
For any population loading matrix compatible with that zero pattern,
\[
  \lambda_{0,g}\T a_f=0\qquad(g\ne f),
\]
so \(T_A=\Lambda_0\T A\) is diagonal. It is nonsingular if
\[
  \lambda_{0,f}\T a_f\ne 0
  \qquad(f=1,\ldots,k).
\]
The original \(A=Z\) is the special case \(a_f\) equal to the indicator vector
of \(\mathcal P_f\), when all assigned indicators are factor-pure. Other
choices include a single marker anchor, equal weights within \(\mathcal P_f\),
or externally fixed reliability weights. Without factor-pure anchors, or
without an oracle loading matrix, a fixed pattern-only \(A\) cannot generally
guarantee \(T_A\) diagonal.

The scale of the raw \(a_f\)'s is immaterial because the actual score
coefficients are standardized:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:standardized-score-coefs}
  \widetilde A(H)
  =
  A\{\diag(A\T H A)\}^{-1/2},
  \qquad
  F_A=\widetilde A(H)\T Y_H .
\end{equation}
Thus the score proxies have unit variance under \(H\). To remove the remaining
sign ambiguity, choose a marker \(m_f\) and orient column \(f\) so that
\[
  \Cov_H(Y_{H,m_f},F_{A,f})\ge 0 .
\]
Equivalently, multiply \(a_f\) by \(-1\) when that covariance is negative.
After this convention, the phrase ``the score defined by \(A\)'' means the
oriented, \(H\)-standardized coefficient matrix \(\widetilde A(H)\).

This general form has a clean coordinate-equivariance property. Let \(L\) be a
nonsingular change of observed coordinates,
\[
  Y_H^\ast=L Y_H,
  \qquad
  H^\ast=LHL\T,
\]
and transform the composite matrix contravariantly:
\[
  A^\ast=L^{-\mathsf T}A .
\]
Then the raw composites are identical:
\[
  {A^\ast}\T Y_H^\ast=A\T Y_H,
\]
and the composite covariance is identical:
\[
  {A^\ast}\T H^\ast A^\ast=A\T H A .
\]
Therefore \(D_{A^\ast}^\ast=D_A\), \(F_{A^\ast}^\ast=F_A\), and
\[
  \Phi_{A^\ast}^\ast=\Phi_A .
\]
The loading matrix is equivariant:
\[
  \Lambda_{A^\ast}^\ast
  =
  H^\ast A^\ast D_A^{-1/2}\Phi_A^{-1}
  =
  L H A D_A^{-1/2}\Phi_A^{-1}
  =
  L\Lambda_A .
\]
Consequently the fitted common covariance transforms as it should:
\[
  \Lambda_{A^\ast}^\ast\Phi_{A^\ast}^\ast
  {\Lambda_{A^\ast}^\ast}\T
  =
  L\Lambda_A\Phi_A\Lambda_A\T L\T .
\]
This is an equivariance statement for the common part. The diagonal residual
variance vector is still coordinate-specific; arbitrary mixing of observed
variables does not preserve the usual CFA requirement that residual covariance
be diagonal. For diagonal rescalings or permutations, the residual diagonal
transforms in the expected coordinate-wise way.

\section{Population loading constraints}

The standardized-composite construction also gives a direct population version
of loading constraints. The important point is that the composites are still
formed first. Thus \(D\), \(F\), and \(\Phi_G\) are fixed by \(H\) and \(Z\).
Only the loading regression is constrained.

Let \(\mathcal L\subset\mathbb R^{p\times k}\) be the loading space allowed by
the population model. This space can encode simple-structure zeros, equality
constraints, or both. The constrained Guttman loading matrix is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:constrained-loading-space}
  \Lambda_{\mathcal L}
  =
  \argmin_{\Lambda\in\mathcal L}
  \E_H\{\lVert Y_H-\Lambda F\rVert^2\}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\[
  \Lambda_{\mathcal L}
  =
  \argmin_{\Lambda\in\mathcal L}
  \left[
    \tr(H)-2\tr\{\Lambda D^{-1/2}Z\T H\}
    +\tr(\Lambda\Phi_G\Lambda\T)
  \right].
\]
After this constrained regression, keep the same standardized-composite
covariance \(\Phi_G\) and set
\[
  \psi_{\mathcal L}
  =
  \diag\{S-\Lambda_{\mathcal L}\Phi_G\Lambda_{\mathcal L}\T\}.
\]
This defines the constrained population map.

For linear equality constraints, vectorize the loading matrix. Let
\[
  \ell=\operatorname{vec}(\Lambda),
  \qquad
  c=\operatorname{vec}(HZD^{-1/2}),
  \qquad
  W=\Phi_G\otimes I_p .
\]
The loading part of the criterion is
\[
  \ell\T W\ell-2c\T\ell
\]
up to constants. The unconstrained solution is
\[
  \ell_G=\operatorname{vec}(\Lambda_G)=W^{-1}c .
\]
For affine equality constraints
\[
  R\ell=r,
\]
the constrained population loading vector is the \(W\)-metric projection
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linear-loading-projection}
  \ell_R
  =
  \ell_G
  -
  W^{-1}R\T(RW^{-1}R\T)^{-1}(R\ell_G-r),
\end{equation}
provided the displayed inverses exist. On a boundary, the same formula can be
read on the estimable subspace with Moore-Penrose inverses, but then the output
should be reported as a boundary convention.

The same construction admits a row-weighted loading regression. Let
\(V_Y\) be a symmetric positive definite metric on the observed variables, and
let \(C_G=HZD^{-1/2}\). Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-loading-space}
  \Lambda_{\mathcal L,V_Y}
  =
  \argmin_{\Lambda\in\mathcal L}
  \E_H\{(Y_H-\Lambda F)\T V_Y(Y_H-\Lambda F)\}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, up to constants,
\[
  \Lambda_{\mathcal L,V_Y}
  =
  \argmin_{\Lambda\in\mathcal L}
  \left[
    \tr(V_Y\Lambda\Phi_G\Lambda\T)
    -2\tr(V_Y\Lambda C_G\T)
  \right].
\]
If \(\mathcal L=\mathbb R^{p\times k}\), the row weight does not change the
solution:
\[
  V_Y(\Lambda\Phi_G-C_G)=0
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  \Lambda=C_G\Phi_G^{-1}.
\]
Thus row weights matter only once the loading space is restricted, or once one
chooses an off-model population target within a restricted loading space.

In vector form, the weighted criterion uses
\[
  W_{V_Y}=\Phi_G\otimes V_Y,
  \qquad
  c_{V_Y}=\operatorname{vec}(V_YC_G).
\]
For affine constraints \(R\ell=r\),
\[
  \ell_{R,V_Y}
  =
  \ell_G
  -
  W_{V_Y}^{-1}R\T(RW_{V_Y}^{-1}R\T)^{-1}(R\ell_G-r).
\]
If the loading space is written as \(\ell=E\beta\), then
\[
  \beta_{\mathcal L,V_Y}
  =
  (E\T W_{V_Y}E)^{-1}E\T c_{V_Y},
  \qquad
  \ell_{\mathcal L,V_Y}=E\beta_{\mathcal L,V_Y}.
\]
At an exact population point with \(\Lambda_0\in\mathcal L\), every positive
definite \(V_Y\) gives the same oriented loading matrix. Away from that
constrained population model, \(V_Y\) selects which weighted projection of the
unconstrained loading regression is called the constrained target.

A tau-equivalent block is the simplest example. For one factor with indicator
set \(I\), the constraint is
\[
  \lambda_i=\alpha\qquad(i\in I).
\]
Since \(\Phi_G=1\) in the one-factor unit-variance case,
\eqref{eq:constrained-loading-space} gives
\[
  \alpha
  =
  |I|^{-1}\sum_{i\in I}\Cov_H(Y_{H,i},F).
\]
For multiple correlated factors, or for tau-equivalence combined with
simple-structure zeros, use the vector formula above. If the allowed loading
space is written as
\[
  \ell=E\beta ,
\]
where \(E\) inserts fixed zeros and repeats equal loadings, then
\[
  \beta_{\mathcal L}
  =
  (E\T W E)^{-1}E\T c,
  \qquad
  \ell_{\mathcal L}=E\beta_{\mathcal L}.
\]

With row weights, the one-factor tau-equivalent average becomes the
\(V_Y\)-weighted projection of \(C_G\) onto the constant-loading vector. If
\(\mathbf 1_I\) is the indicator vector for the block and \(V_{Y,I}\) is the
corresponding principal submatrix, then
\[
  \alpha_{V_Y}
  =
  (\mathbf 1_I\T V_{Y,I}\mathbf 1_I)^{-1}
  \mathbf 1_I\T V_{Y,I}C_{G,I}.
\]

These constraints are scale-dependent. The formulas above define equality in
the unit-variance, standardized-composite orientation. Converting the result to
first-loading-equals-one units is still allowed, but equality of loadings need
not mean the same thing after that diagonal reparameterization. Constraints on
\(\Phi\) or on residual variances are different objects; this section only
constrains the loading regression while keeping \(\Phi_G\) fixed as the
standardized composite covariance.

At an exact population point whose unit-variance loading matrix already lies in
\(\mathcal L\), the constrained map returns the same oriented loading matrix as
the unconstrained map. Away from that constrained population model, it returns
the closest allowed loading matrix in the composite-regression criterion.

There is no universally best row weight as a population map under
misspecification; different \(V_Y\)'s define different off-model targets. For
estimation, the efficiency-optimal weight is instead a sampling statement. If
\(\hat\ell_G\) has asymptotic covariance \(\Omega_\ell\), the optimal
minimum-distance estimator under \(\ell=E\beta\) uses
\(\Omega_\ell^{+}\), giving
\[
  \hat\beta_{\mathrm{MD}}
  =
  (E\T\Omega_\ell^{+}E)^{-1}E\T\Omega_\ell^{+}\hat\ell_G .
\]
The matrix \(\Omega_\ell\) depends on the full map from the sample covariance
to \(H\), \(D\), \(\Phi_G\), and \(\Lambda_G\). In particular, it depends on the
chosen communality estimator and on the fourth-moment behavior of the data.
The optimal minimum-distance weight therefore cannot be determined from the
population pair \((H,Z)\) alone, and it is not generally restricted to the
Kronecker row-weight form \(\Phi_G\otimes V_Y\).

\section{Identification charts and moving composites}

The previous section writes constraints in the unit-variance chart, where the
standardized composites \(F\) have covariance \(\Phi_G\) with unit diagonal.
This is only a chart choice. The composite scores need not be fixed across
charts; what should be fixed is the prediction \( \Lambda F \), or equivalently
the fitted common covariance.

Let \(F_0\) be any \(k\)-vector of auxiliary score proxies, with covariance
\(\Phi_0=\Cov(F_0)\), and define
\[
  Q_{V_Y}(\Lambda;F_0)
  =
  \E_H\{(Y_H-\Lambda F_0)\T V_Y(Y_H-\Lambda F_0)\}.
\]
For a nonsingular latent-coordinate change \(T\), set
\[
  F_T=T^{-1}F_0,
  \qquad
  \Gamma=\Lambda T .
\]
Then \(\Gamma F_T=\Lambda F_0\), and
\[
  \Phi_T=\Cov(F_T)=T^{-1}\Phi_0T^{-\mathsf T},
  \qquad
  \Gamma\Phi_T\Gamma\T=\Lambda\Phi_0\Lambda\T .
\]
Thus the score basis may move with the loading coordinates without changing
the \(Y_H\)-space object being fitted.

\begin{proposition}[Chart equivariance of the constrained regression]
\label{prop:chart-equivariance}
Let \(V_Y\) be positive definite, let \(F_0\) have finite second moments, and
let \(T\) be nonsingular. For any loading space
\(\mathcal L\subset\mathbb R^{p\times k}\), define
\[
  \mathcal L_T=\{\Lambda T:\Lambda\in\mathcal L\}.
\]
Then
\[
  Q_{V_Y}(\Lambda T;T^{-1}F_0)
  =
  Q_{V_Y}(\Lambda;F_0)
  \qquad(\Lambda\in\mathcal L).
\]
Consequently the minimizer set for the transformed problem is the transformed
minimizer set:
\[
  \argmin_{\Gamma\in\mathcal L_T}
    Q_{V_Y}(\Gamma;T^{-1}F_0)
  =
  \left\{\Lambda T:
    \Lambda\in\argmin_{\Lambda\in\mathcal L}Q_{V_Y}(\Lambda;F_0)
  \right\}.
\]
If the minimizer is unique, \(\Gamma^\star=\Lambda^\star T\). The fitted
prediction and fitted common covariance are unchanged.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For \(\Gamma=\Lambda T\) and \(F_T=T^{-1}F_0\),
\[
  Y_H-\Gamma F_T=Y_H-\Lambda T T^{-1}F_0=Y_H-\Lambda F_0 .
\]
The objective values are therefore identical point by point. The displayed
identity for the argmin sets follows from the bijection
\(\Lambda\mapsto\Lambda T\) between \(\mathcal L\) and \(\mathcal L_T\). The
common-covariance statement follows from
\[
  \Gamma\Cov(F_T)\Gamma\T
  =
  \Lambda T(T^{-1}\Phi_0T^{-\mathsf T})T\T\Lambda\T
  =
  \Lambda\Phi_0\Lambda\T .
\]
\end{proof}

If \(T\) is fixed, affine loading constraints remain affine. For example, if
\(\ell=\operatorname{vec}(\Lambda)\) and the original chart has
\[
  R\ell=r,
\]
then \(\ell_T=\operatorname{vec}(\Lambda T)=(T\T\otimes I_p)\ell\), and the
same feasible set in the transformed chart is
\[
  R(T^{-\mathsf T}\otimes I_p)\ell_T=r .
\]
Thus a fixed scale conversion never explains nonlinearity. The nonlinearity
appears when the scale conversion itself is determined by the loading matrix.

A more invariant way to write a chart-specific constraint is to optimize over
the chart and the latent-coordinate change together. Let
\(\mathcal C\subset\mathbb R^{p\times k}\) be the loading space in the chosen
chart, and let \(\mathcal T\) be a set of nonsingular coordinate changes. Then
\[
  \min_{\Gamma\in\mathcal C,\ T\in\mathcal T}
    Q_{V_Y}(\Gamma;T^{-1}F_0)
\]
is exactly the same prediction problem as
\[
  \min_{\Lambda\in\mathcal L_0(\mathcal C,\mathcal T)}
    Q_{V_Y}(\Lambda;F_0),
  \qquad
  \mathcal L_0(\mathcal C,\mathcal T)
  =
  \{\Gamma T^{-1}:\Gamma\in\mathcal C,\ T\in\mathcal T\}.
\]
The set \(\mathcal C\) can be linear while
\(\mathcal L_0(\mathcal C,\mathcal T)\) is nonlinear. This is not a defect of
the weight \(V_Y\); it is the algebra of the identification chart.

\begin{example}[Marker and unit-variance charts]
Let \(F_u\) be the unit-variance score basis, so
\(\diag\{\Cov(F_u)\}=I\). A marker chart with marker \(m_f\) for factor \(f\)
can be written as
\[
  \Gamma=\Lambda_u M^{-1},
  \qquad
  F_m=MF_u,
  \qquad
  \Phi_m=M\Phi_uM,
\]
where
\[
  M=\diag\{(\Lambda_u)_{m_1,1},\ldots,(\Lambda_u)_{m_k,k}\}.
\]
Then \(\Gamma F_m=\Lambda_uF_u\), and the marker loadings satisfy
\(\Gamma_{m_f f}=1\).

If \(M\) is a fixed diagonal matrix, Proposition
\ref{prop:chart-equivariance} applies directly and all affine constraints stay
affine after transformation. In the usual marker convention, however, \(M\)
depends on the loading matrix being represented. Pulling marker-linear
constraints back to unit-variance coordinates can therefore change their
algebra.

For indicators on the same factor,
\[
  \Gamma_{if}=\Gamma_{jf}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  (\Lambda_u)_{if}=(\Lambda_u)_{jf},
\]
so within-factor equality is linear in both charts. A fixed marker-scale
loading value is also linear after pullback:
\[
  \Gamma_{if}=c
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  (\Lambda_u)_{if}=c(\Lambda_u)_{m_f f}.
\]
By contrast, an equality across two differently scaled factors gives
\[
  \Gamma_{if}=\Gamma_{jg}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \frac{(\Lambda_u)_{if}}{(\Lambda_u)_{m_f f}}
  =
  \frac{(\Lambda_u)_{jg}}{(\Lambda_u)_{m_g g}},
\]
or equivalently
\[
  (\Lambda_u)_{if}(\Lambda_u)_{m_g g}
  =
  (\Lambda_u)_{jg}(\Lambda_u)_{m_f f}.
\]
This is bilinear in the unit-variance loadings. A row metric \(V_Y\) can change
which off-model projection is preferred, but it cannot make this pullback
linear.

For a marker-linear space \(\mathcal C_m\), the corresponding moving-score
formulation is
\[
  \min_{\Gamma\in\mathcal C_m,\ M}
    \E_H\{(Y_H-\Gamma M F_u)\T V_Y(Y_H-\Gamma M F_u)\},
\]
with \(M\) restricted to nonsingular diagonal matrices satisfying the desired
orientation convention. In unit-variance coordinates this is the same as
minimizing over the generally nonlinear set
\[
  \{\Gamma M:\Gamma\in\mathcal C_m,\ M
    \hbox{ nonsingular diagonal}\}.
\]
Thus the same population target can be written as a linear constraint in the
marker chart, or as a nonlinear constraint in the unit-variance chart, provided
the score basis is transformed with the chart.
\end{example}

The lavaanified partable gives an algorithmic version of this chart choice.
Raw syntax is not enough, because defaults such as marker selection,
\texttt{std.lv}, effect coding, group options, and user labels determine the
actual fixed rows and equality rows.
Let \(\vartheta\) denote the lavaan parameter vector extracted from
\(\Lambda\), \(\Phi\), and \(\psi\); this is not the residual covariance
matrix. Suppose the partable supplies linear equality constraints
\[
  R\vartheta=r .
\]
Start from an oriented base representative
\((\Lambda_0,\Phi_0,\psi_0)\), and suppose the score proxies are written
\[
  F_0=B_0\T Y_H .
\]
For a simple CFA loading pattern with named factors, the admissible
latent-coordinate changes are diagonal after signs have been oriented. Write
\[
  T(x)=\diag(e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_k}).
\]
The diagonal orbit is
\[
  \Lambda(x)=\Lambda_0T(x),
  \qquad
  \Phi(x)=T(x)^{-1}\Phi_0T(x)^{-\mathsf T},
  \qquad
  B(x)=B_0T(x)^{-\mathsf T}.
\]
Then \(B(x)\T Y_H=T(x)^{-1}F_0\), so this is exactly the moving-score
transformation in Proposition \ref{prop:chart-equivariance}. Let
\[
  \vartheta(x)=q\{\Lambda(x),\Phi(x),\psi_0\},
  \qquad
  g(x)=R\vartheta(x)-r,
\]
where \(q\) is the partable extraction map. Constructing the lavaan chart from
first principles means solving selected rows of \(g(x)=0\) for the scale
coordinates \(x\).

\begin{proposition}[Diagonal chart solve from linear partable constraints]
\label{prop:lavaan-chart-solve}
Let \(C\) select \(k\) rows of \(R\), and write
\[
  g_C(x)=C\{R\vartheta(x)-r\}.
\]
If there is a solution \(x^\star\) with
\[
  g_C(x^\star)=0,
  \qquad
  \rank\left\{
    \frac{\partial g_C}{\partial x\T}(x^\star)
  \right\}=k,
\]
then \(T(x^\star)\) is the locally unique diagonal representative in the
orbit satisfying those chart equations. The corresponding lavaan-chart
representative is
\[
  \Lambda_{\mathrm{lav}}=\Lambda_0T(x^\star),
  \qquad
  \Phi_{\mathrm{lav}}=T(x^\star)^{-1}\Phi_0T(x^\star)^{-\mathsf T},
  \qquad
  B_{\mathrm{lav}}=B_0T(x^\star)^{-\mathsf T}.
\]
The remaining rows of \(R\vartheta=r\) are then checked or imposed in that
chart; they are not automatically chart equations.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The map \(x\mapsto T(x)\) parameterizes the oriented diagonal coordinate
orbit. The rank condition and the implicit function theorem give a locally
isolated solution \(x^\star\) to the selected equations on that orbit. The
matrix formulas are exactly the latent-coordinate action above, and
Proposition \ref{prop:chart-equivariance} gives the corresponding moving-score
interpretation.
\end{proof}

This proposition is deliberately local. If no \(k\)-row selection has full
rank, the linear partable constraints do not fully fix the factor scales. If
the selected equations have no solution on the orbit, they are inconsistent
with that base representative. If additional rows fail after the chart solve,
those rows are substantive constraints on the model target, not scale
normalizations to be absorbed into \(T\).

\begin{example}[Common lavaan chart equations]
For a loading on factor \(f\),
\[
  \lambda_{if}(x)=(\Lambda_0)_{if}e^{x_f}.
\]
For a latent covariance,
\[
  \phi_{fg}(x)=
  e^{-x_f}(\Phi_0)_{fg}e^{-x_g}.
\]
Thus the common chart equations are obtained directly from the partable rows:
\[
  \lambda_{m_f f}(x)=1
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  e^{x_f}=1/(\Lambda_0)_{m_f f},
\]
\[
  \phi_{ff}(x)=1
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  e^{x_f}=\{(\Phi_0)_{ff}\}^{1/2},
\]
and, for effect coding within factor \(f\),
\[
  \sum_i a_i\lambda_{if}(x)=c
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  e^{x_f}=
  \frac{c}{\sum_i a_i(\Lambda_0)_{if}} .
\]
Each row fixes one scale direction when the displayed denominator is nonzero
and the sign convention is compatible.

A same-factor equality does not fix scale:
\[
  \lambda_{if}(x)=\lambda_{jf}(x)
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  (\Lambda_0)_{if}=(\Lambda_0)_{jf}.
\]
If the equality is true, the whole diagonal orbit satisfies it; if it is false,
no diagonal scaling can make it true. It is therefore a substantive loading
constraint, not a chart equation.

A cross-factor equality can fix relative scale:
\[
  \lambda_{if}(x)=\lambda_{jg}(x)
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  (\Lambda_0)_{if}e^{x_f}
  =
  (\Lambda_0)_{jg}e^{x_g}.
\]
When the two base loadings have compatible signs, this gives
\[
  x_f-x_g
  =
  \log |(\Lambda_0)_{jg}|
  -
  \log |(\Lambda_0)_{if}|.
\]
Such rows can help connect factor scales, but they do not by themselves fix
the absolute scale of every factor. Extra cross-factor equalities may also
overdetermine the orbit. The rank test in Proposition
\ref{prop:lavaan-chart-solve} is the implementation check.
\end{example}

\section{Observed input canonicalization}

The row metric \(V_Y\) can also be read as an input standardization. This is
often the better interpretation. The question is not only how residuals are
weighted after the fact, but which observed coordinates are used before the
composite regression is defined.

Let \(G\succ0\) be a declared observed-coordinate scale matrix, and write
\[
  W_G=G^{-1/2},
  \qquad
  X_G=W_GY_H,
  \qquad
  H_G=\Cov(X_G)=W_GHW_G .
\]
For score coefficients \(B_G\in\mathbb R^{p\times k}\), define
\[
  F_G=B_G\T X_G,
  \qquad
  \Phi_G^B=B_G\T H_GB_G .
\]
The Euclidean input-space regression is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:input-space-regression}
  \Gamma_G
  =
  \argmin_{\Gamma}
  \E_H\{\lVert X_G-\Gamma F_G\rVert^2\}.
\end{equation}
When \(\Phi_G^B\) is nonsingular,
\[
  \Gamma_G=H_GB_G(\Phi_G^B)^{-1}.
\]
Returning to the original \(Y_H\)-coordinates gives
\[
  \Lambda_G^{(Y)}=G^{1/2}\Gamma_G .
\]
Thus \eqref{eq:input-space-regression} is exactly the weighted regression
\[
  \E_H\{(Y_H-\Lambda_G^{(Y)}F_G)\T
        G^{-1}
        (Y_H-\Lambda_G^{(Y)}F_G)\}.
\]
So choosing \(V_Y=G^{-1}\) and choosing inputs \(X_G=G^{-1/2}Y_H\) are the same
population target. The input formulation is preferable when \(G\) is intended
as a canonicalization of the observed variables.

The conservative choice is diagonal standardization. Let \(\Sigma_Y\) denote
the population covariance of the observed variables before the communality
replacement, and set
\[
  G_{\mathrm{diag}}=\diag(\Sigma_Y).
\]
Then
\[
  X_{\mathrm{diag}}=G_{\mathrm{diag}}^{-1/2}Y_H
\]
gives the standardized-residual criterion
\[
  \E_H\left[
    \sum_{i=1}^p
    \frac{(Y_{H,i}-(\Lambda F)_i)^2}{(\Sigma_Y)_{ii}}
  \right].
\]
This is invariant to changing the measurement units of named indicators, as
long as \(G_{\mathrm{diag}}\) is recomputed after the unit change. It is the
most lavaan-compatible canonicalization because it keeps the named indicator
coordinates and all partable constraints in their usual coordinates.

The stronger choice is block whitening. Suppose the simple CFA pattern
partitions the indicators into blocks
\[
  P_1,\ldots,P_k,
\]
where \(P_f\) contains the indicators assigned to factor \(f\). Define
\[
  G_{\mathrm{blk}}
  =
  \bigoplus_{f=1}^k
  (\Sigma_Y)_{P_fP_f}.
\]
Then \(X_{\mathrm{blk}}=G_{\mathrm{blk}}^{-1/2}Y_H\) whitens each indicator
block with respect to the observed covariance scale. This is the natural
canonicalization for transformations that only mix indicators assigned to the
same factor.

\begin{proposition}[Block whitening leaves only orthogonal block freedom]
\label{prop:block-whitening}
Let
\[
  L=\bigoplus_{f=1}^k L_f
\]
be nonsingular, with each \(L_f\) acting only inside block \(P_f\). Let
\[
  Y_H^\ast=LY_H,
  \qquad
  H^\ast=LHL\T,
  \qquad
  G_{\mathrm{blk}}^\ast=LG_{\mathrm{blk}}L\T .
\]
With \(X=G_{\mathrm{blk}}^{-1/2}Y_H\) and
\[
  X^\ast=(G_{\mathrm{blk}}^\ast)^{-1/2}Y_H^\ast,
\]
there is a block-orthogonal matrix \(O\) such that
\[
  X^\ast=OX .
\]
One such matrix is
\[
  O=(G_{\mathrm{blk}}^\ast)^{-1/2}LG_{\mathrm{blk}}^{1/2}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The displayed \(O\) satisfies
\[
  O O\T
  =
  (G_{\mathrm{blk}}^\ast)^{-1/2}
  LG_{\mathrm{blk}}L\T
  (G_{\mathrm{blk}}^\ast)^{-1/2}
  =
  I .
\]
Also
\[
  OX
  =
  (G_{\mathrm{blk}}^\ast)^{-1/2}LG_{\mathrm{blk}}^{1/2}
  G_{\mathrm{blk}}^{-1/2}Y_H
  =
  (G_{\mathrm{blk}}^\ast)^{-1/2}LY_H
  =
  X^\ast .
\]
\end{proof}

Block whitening makes Euclidean loss invariant to the remaining
block-orthogonal freedom, provided the score rule moves with that freedom. A
score rule \(B(H_G)\) is block-orthogonally equivariant if
\[
  B(O H_G O\T)=O B(H_G)
\]
for every block-orthogonal \(O\) in the allowed class. Then the scores are
unchanged:
\[
  B(O H_G O\T)\T OX=B(H_G)\T X .
\]
Consequently the unconstrained regression satisfies
\[
  \Gamma^\ast=O\Gamma,
  \qquad
  \Phi^{B,\ast}=\Phi^B,
\]
and the fitted common covariance in the original observed coordinates
transforms as \(L(\Lambda\Phi^B\Lambda\T)L\T\). The same statement holds for
constrained regressions when the loading space is transformed as
\(\mathcal C^\ast=\{O\Gamma:\Gamma\in\mathcal C\}\), with latent chart changes
handled by Proposition \ref{prop:chart-equivariance}.

The fixed incidence score \(Z\) is not block-orthogonally equivariant in
general. It is tied to named indicators. A loading-aligned score rule is
equivariant. If an input-space loading matrix \(\Gamma\) has full column rank,
the oracle score coefficient
\[
  B_\Gamma=\Gamma(\Gamma\T\Gamma)^{-1}
\]
satisfies
\[
  B_{O\Gamma}
  =
  O\Gamma\{(O\Gamma)\T(O\Gamma)\}^{-1}
  =
  OB_\Gamma .
\]
This is not directly an estimator because \(\Gamma\) is unknown, but it
describes the canonical class: block-whiten the inputs, construct scores by a
loading-aligned equivariant rule, and then run the Euclidean composite
regression in the canonical coordinates. A practical version can use a
two-step rule, first obtaining a named-coordinate Guttman loading matrix and
then constructing \(B_\Gamma\) from that preliminary representative.

These two canonicalizations have different status. The diagonal version is a
safe default for a lavaan-like named-indicator estimator. It solves the
high-variance-variable problem while keeping ordinary partable constraints
intact. The block-whitened version is the more ambitious invariant target for
a recommended Guttman class, but it requires the full map \(S\mapsto H\mapsto
B\) to be designed for block equivariance. The current Spearman-Harman
communality step and the fixed \(Z\) scores are named-coordinate constructions,
so they should not be advertised as invariant to arbitrary within-block
linear mixing.

\section{What is and is not minimized}

The preceding lemmas give the clean answer. The Guttman construction minimizes
the composite prediction loss \eqref{eq:composite-objective}. It does not generally
minimize an SEM covariance discrepancy
\[
  F_V(\theta)
  =
  \{\vech(S)-\sigma(\theta)\}\T
  V
  \{\vech(S)-\sigma(\theta)\},
\]
where \(V\) is the ULS, GLS, WLS, or NTML weight. A minimizer of \(F_V\) has the
first-order condition
\[
  \Delta(\theta)\T V
  \{\vech(S)-\sigma(\theta)\}=0,
\]
or the equivalent NTML score condition when \(V\) is the likelihood weight. The
Guttman estimate is obtained from \(H\), \(Z\), and the standardized composite
regression above; there is no reason for it to satisfy that residual
orthogonality condition except in special cases.

Thus NTML and ULS are fine as \emph{post-estimation residual statistics}. They
are not the discrepancies that define the point estimate.

\section{Boundary-complete extension}

Equations \eqref{eq:LambdaG} and \eqref{eq:common-proj} use \(D^{-1/2}\) and
the inverse of the standardized composite covariance \(\Phi_G\), so the
interior construction is not automatically total. A boundary-complete version
should distinguish three cases.

First, if the diagonal entries of \(D\) are positive and \(\Phi_G\) is positive
definite, use the ordinary Guttman map above.

Second, if the diagonal entries of \(D\) are positive and \(\Phi_G\) is
positive semidefinite but singular, use the Moore-Penrose inverse when the
covariance equations are compatible:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compat}
  HZD^{-1/2}=HZD^{-1/2}\Phi_G^{+}\Phi_G .
\end{equation}
Then
\[
  \Lambda_{\mathrm{MP}}=HZD^{-1/2}\Phi_G^{+}
\]
is the unique minimum-Frobenius-norm minimizer of
\eqref{eq:composite-objective}. It is also the ridge limit
\[
  \lim_{\lambda\downarrow0}HZD^{-1/2}(\Phi_G+\lambda I)^{-1}.
\]
All minimizers give the same fitted common covariance,
\[
  \Lambda\Phi_G\Lambda\T
  =
  HZD^{-1/2}\Phi_G^{+}D^{-1/2}Z\T H ,
\]
so the common covariance is identified even though the coordinate
representation of the factors is not.

The parameter split is the difficult part. A singular \(\Phi_G\) means that the
standardized composite space has rank below \(k\). The common covariance still
has a canonical Moore-Penrose fitted value, but the \(k\)-factor unit-variance
representative is on the boundary because \(\Phi_G\) is singular. A
boundary-complete implementation should therefore return the common covariance
and the Moore-Penrose representative with an explicit boundary warning, and
should not present the individual factor parameters as regularly identified.

Third, if some diagonal entry of \(D\) is nonpositive, if \(\Phi_G\) is
indefinite, or if \eqref{eq:compat} fails, the quadratic
\eqref{eq:composite-objective} has no pure standardized-composite
least-squares solution. An always-returning estimator can still be defined by a
named regularized mode, for example
\[
  \Lambda_{\lambda}=HZD^{-1/2}(\Phi_G+\lambda I)^{-1},
\]
with \(\lambda\) chosen to make the system positive definite, or by first
projecting the standardized composite covariance to a positive semidefinite
matrix. If \(D^{-1/2}\) itself is the failure point, the regularized variant
must also name how composite variances are floored or repaired. This is useful
engineering, but it should be reported as a regularized Guttman variant, not as
the clean population map.

\section{Residual statistics at the Guttman estimate}

Let \(s=\vech(S)\), let \(\hat\theta_G=\tau_G(s)\), and let
\[
  r=s-\sigma(\hat\theta_G).
\]
For a fixed or model-implied weight \(V\), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:TV}
  T_V=N r\T V r .
\end{equation}
This includes ULS with the vech metric and the NTML residual quadratic form with
the model-implied normal-theory weight evaluated at
\(\Sigma(\hat\theta_G)\).

Let
\[
  J_G=\frac{\partial\tau_G}{\partial s\T},
  \qquad
  \Delta=\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial\theta\T}
\]
at the target point. Under exact fit, Fisher consistency gives
\[
  J_G\Delta=I .
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:resid-projector}
  \sqrt N\,r
  =
  M_G\,\sqrt N(s-\sigma_0)+o_p(1),
  \qquad
  M_G=I-\Delta J_G .
\end{equation}
The matrix \(M_G\) is a residual projector, but it is generally an oblique
projector. It is not the \(V\)-orthogonal residual projector of the
\(F_V\)-minimizer.

Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixture}
  T_V
  \Rightarrow
  \sum_j \lambda_j \chi^2_{1,j},
\end{equation}
where the nonzero \(\lambda_j\)'s are the eigenvalues of
\[
  (M_G\T V M_G)\Gamma .
\]
Only when \(J_G\) equals the \(V\)-least-squares Jacobian and \(V\) is the
matching inverse moment covariance does this collapse to an ordinary
\(\chi^2_{df}\). That is the efficient ML or GLS special case, not the Guttman
case.

For nested models, the LRT-shaped difference
\[
  T_d=T_{V,0}-T_{V,1}
\]
is therefore a pseudo-LRT. Its reference is obtained from the difference of the
two quadratic forms. Since neither fitted value is required to minimize
\(F_V\), \(T_d\) is not guaranteed to be nonnegative in finite samples.

\section{Multi-sample blocks and the native constrained test}

For independent sample groups \(g=1,\ldots,G\), apply the same Guttman map to
each covariance block:
\[
  \hat\theta_g=\tau_G(s_g),
  \qquad
  J_g=\frac{\partial\tau_G}{\partial s_g\T}.
\]
Stack \(\hat\theta=(\hat\theta_1\T,\ldots,\hat\theta_G\T)\T\). If
\(\Gamma_g\) is the fourth-moment covariance of \(s_g\), then the finite-sample
delta covariance of the stacked estimator is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Omega-block}
  \Omega
  =
  \bigoplus_{g=1}^G
  \frac{1}{N_g}J_g\Gamma_gJ_g\T .
\end{equation}
The configural Guttman estimator imposes no cross-block equality constraints.
It simply estimates every block independently and stacks the results.

Now impose a linear invariance constraint
\[
  R\theta=c
\]
on the stacked parameter vector. The natural constrained Guttman estimator is
the minimum-distance projection
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projection}
  \tilde\theta
  =
  \hat\theta
  -
  \Omega R\T(R\Omega R\T)^{-1}(R\hat\theta-c).
\end{equation}
It is the solution of
\[
  \tilde\theta
  =
  \argmin_{\eta:\ R\eta=c}
  (\hat\theta-\eta)\T\Omega^{+}(\hat\theta-\eta),
\]
with the usual interpretation on the estimable constraint subspace.

The associated statistic is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wald}
  W
  =
  (R\hat\theta-c)\T
  (R\Omega R\T)^{-1}
  (R\hat\theta-c).
\end{equation}
Under the null, \(W\Rightarrow\chi^2_{\rank(R)}\). This is the native
Guttman discrepancy for invariance work: distance in the estimator's own
asymptotic parameter metric. It is monotone, closed form, and does not require
pretending that the point estimate minimized NTML or ULS.

\section{Audit checklist}

For one covariance block, the implementation can be checked in this order:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Build the incidence matrix \(Z\) from the simple CFA loading pattern.
\item If using the generalized score-proxy version, replace \(Z\) by a fixed
  composite matrix \(A\), either from factor-pure anchor sets or from another
  declared score rule. Use the oriented standardized coefficient matrix
  \(\widetilde A(H)=A\{\diag(A\T H A)\}^{-1/2}\) as the actual score. Under a
  coordinate change \(Y^\ast=LY\), use \(A^\ast=L^{-\mathsf T}A\) to preserve
  the same auxiliary composites. Read the remaining \(Z\)-formulas with \(A\)
  substituted.
\item For each factor block, compute the Spearman-Harman communalities
  \eqref{eq:spearman-h}; place them on the diagonal to get \(H\).
\item If using an observed-input canonicalization, choose \(G\) and work with
  \(X_G=G^{-1/2}Y_H\) and \(H_G=G^{-1/2}HG^{-1/2}\). The conservative choice is
  \(G_{\mathrm{diag}}=\diag(\Sigma_Y)\). The stronger block-aligned choice is
  \(G_{\mathrm{blk}}=\bigoplus_f(\Sigma_Y)_{P_fP_f}\), but then the score rule
  \(B\) should be block-orthogonally equivariant. Convert input-space loadings
  back by \(\Lambda^{(Y)}=G^{1/2}\Gamma\). In this branch, replace the next
  two \(Z,H\)-formulas by
  \(\Phi^B=B\T H_GB\) and \(\Gamma=H_GB(\Phi^B)^{-1}\), with optional score
  standardization if unit-variance score coordinates are desired.
\item Form \(D=\diag(Z\T HZ)\) and the standardized composite covariance
  \(\Phi_G=D^{-1/2}(Z\T HZ)D^{-1/2}\).
\item Solve \(\Lambda_G=HZD^{-1/2}\Phi_G^{-1}\). This is the loading matrix
  minimizing the composite prediction loss when \(\Phi_G\) is positive
  definite.
\item If the population model imposes loading constraints, replace the previous
  solve by the constrained regression in
  \eqref{eq:constrained-loading-space}, or by the weighted version
  \eqref{eq:weighted-loading-space} if a row metric \(V_Y\) is part of the
  estimator definition.
\item If the constraints are stated in another identification chart, transform
  the score basis with that chart as in Proposition
  \ref{prop:chart-equivariance}. For marker-linear constraints, decide whether
  the marker scaling is fixed after an unconstrained solve or is a nuisance
  scale in the constrained problem. The latter gives the moving-score
  formulation in the marker example and may be nonlinear in unit-variance
  loading coordinates.
\item For lavaanified linear constraints \(R\vartheta=r\), construct the chart
  scale by the orbit solve in Proposition \ref{prop:lavaan-chart-solve}: form
  \(\vartheta(x)=q\{\Lambda T(x),T(x)^{-1}\Phi T(x)^{-\mathsf T},\psi\}\),
  choose scale-fixing rows with rank \(k\), and check the remaining rows as
  substantive constraints in the selected chart.
\item Compute \(\psi=\diag(S-\Lambda\Phi_G\Lambda\T)\), using either the
  unconstrained \(\Lambda_G\) or the constrained
  \(\Lambda_{\mathcal L}\).
\item If first-loading-equals-one output is desired, convert the
  unit-variance matrices by the marker loading matrix \(M_{\mathrm{fl}}\).
\item Assemble the model's free parameter vector from the allowed loading cells,
  latent covariance cells, and residual variances. In the unconstrained
  regression, off-pattern entries of \(\Lambda_G\) are auxiliary unless the
  model itself allows those loadings.
\item For standard errors or tests, finite-difference the full map to obtain
  \(J_G\), then use \(J_G\Gamma J_G\T/N\) for parameter covariance and
  \(M_G=I-\Delta J_G\) for residual goodness-of-fit references.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Summary}

The Guttman estimator has a real least-squares criterion, but it is not the SEM
criterion used by ML, ULS, GLS, or WLS. It first constructs fixed indicator
composites, regresses the observed variables on those composites under the
communality-filled covariance \(H\), and reads the unit-variance latent
covariance as the correlation matrix of the standardized composites.
Replacing \(Z\) by a general composite matrix \(A\) gives fixed score proxies
in the \(H\)-world. Factor-pure anchor sets give pattern-only choices of \(A\),
and the standardized coefficient matrix \(\widetilde A(H)\) defines the actual
score scale. The common part is equivariant when \(A\) is transformed
contravariantly with the observed coordinates.
Observed-row weights are equivalently input canonicalizations:
\(V_Y=G^{-1}\) means running ordinary Euclidean regression after
\(X_G=G^{-1/2}Y_H\). Diagonal standardization gives a safe named-indicator
default. Block whitening gives a stronger invariant target under
within-factor block transformations, provided the score rule is also
block-orthogonally equivariant; fixed \(Z\) is not enough for that stronger
property.
Loading equalities, including tau-equivalence, can be imposed directly as
constraints on that population regression; row weights define different
off-model constrained targets. Identification changes are handled by moving
the score basis with the loading chart. A fixed latent-coordinate change
preserves affine constraints, while marker-style scalings that depend on the
loading matrix can make the same constraint linear in one chart and nonlinear
in another. For lavaanified linear constraints, the diagonal chart is obtained
by solving the scale orbit equations \(R\vartheta(x)=r\) and requiring a
rank-\(k\) chart Jacobian; remaining rows are substantive constraints in that
chart. First-loading-equals-one output is a diagonal reparameterization of the
same fitted common part. Residual NTML or ULS
statistics are useful diagnostics and valid pseudo-tests once their
weighted-\(\chi^2\) reference is corrected with
\(M_G=I-\Delta J_G\). For multi-sample invariance, the more natural test is the
minimum-distance/Wald statistic based on the block-diagonal Guttman delta
covariance.
